    How it works

    17 checks look for the patterns the FTC and university fraud offices warn about: fake checks, fees to start, chat-app interviews, requests for ID or bank details, money and package forwarding, personal or lookalike email addresses, requests to move to your personal email or to text, and pressure to hurry. Every check is a plain rule that runs in your browser. There is no AI and no server, so the message never leaves your device.

    It finds signals, not proof. A clean result means none of the usual tricks showed up. Still apply through the company's own careers site, and never pay or deposit anything to get a job.

    What testing changed

    Before calling v1 done, I ran it on real messages: scam emails that students actually received, from Cornell IT's public Phish Bowl, and real postings from 16 companies' public job boards.

    What broke in v1. Most student scams are a fake professor offering "$600 weekly" for a few remote hours, asking you to text them or reply from your personal Gmail instead of your school email. v1 had no rule for any of that. And every false alarm came from a company's own anti-fraud notice: "we will never ask you to deposit a check" read as a request to deposit one.

    What v2 changed. Words inside a warning against scams no longer count, and the result says which ones it skipped. New checks catch requests to leave your school email, weekly pay, and texting or Instagram contact. Highlights are now numbered in reading order.

    Limits: the scam emails come from one university and many share one template, some have addresses redacted, and two had no text at all (image or attachment only). Job postings are not recruiter emails. A scammer who rewords everything can get past plain rules.
